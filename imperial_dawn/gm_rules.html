Starting off as a Game Master

This section assumes that you've read the player rules. You'll need to at least read the basics and skim the rest to take on this role.

Taking on the role of Game Master is a unique challenge. It takes as much time as you're willing to put into it, and takes a blend of both planning and improvisation. Everyone has a different style of running a game, so do your best to learn from others when you can, but feel free to do whatever you and your players enjoy. So long as you let the players play their characters with enough agency, your plans will never play out perfectly, you will always be at your limits, and the game will be better for it. The truth is that the time and stress and creative energy that you put into your game will almost always be returned to you many times over, and the stories that you tell can be fond memories for decades to come.

This section is intended to get you off the ground as an Imperial Dawn GM. It has basic rules that you will need, and some guidance for how to handle this system in particular. Once you are done with this, my best advice to you is to simply start playing. You'll learn how the system feels quickly, and you'll be better every session. Your players are your friends, they will forgive mistakes. If you don't feel comfortable with that, search around on the internet for more guides on how to be a better GM. A lot of it won't work for you, but try out what you learn when you can, and it will help you to grow more confidently.

Always remember that the masters are still learning.

The Role of Game Master

The role of game master is typically taken by only one person, and that person is responsible for every aspect of the game world that is not the player's characters. The game master therefore writes the world ahead of time, sometimes riffing off of an established setting or work of fiction and sometimes not, and controls all of the non-player characters in the world. They also interpret the results of dice rolls, and are the final arbiter of any disagreement. This is a lot of responsibility, and it must be used well.

Player Characters

The Player Characters, often called PCs, should be the main drivers of the story that you are about to tell. Their unique abilities, personalities, and quirks drive the story forward. Your job is to craft a scenario in which their characters can experience a great story.

The Player's Minions

Players will occasionally pick up followers throughout the course of their adventures, due to abilities they take, or benefits that you see fit to grant them in response to their actions within the story. Pay careful attention to how the players treat their minions. Are they utterly disposable? Are they a flock to be protected? Is the player planning on drawing their next character from their minions in the event of their untimely death?

The Dice

You roll dice just like the players do. By default we recommend that you roll your dice in front of the players, and that you don't fudge the dice at all. This gives the players insight into how many dice are rolled, which can be a general replacement for their characters seeing other character's blinding speed or radiant charm in the game world. A good exception to this is when player characters would not be aware of a roll, or would not observe the results of the roll, such as when someone is lying to them, or sneaking past them. You should make a practice of rolling dice somewhere that they cannot see whenever the players suspect deception, even if there is nothing to hide. This prevents players from metagaming based on whether or not you roll. Rolls in combat almost never fit these criteria, and should almost always occur in the open.

Some GMs prefer to roll behind a screen, so as to conceal more information from the players, and so that they can alter the results of rolls that they do not feel lead to a good experience. Gary Gygax, one of the founders of the genre has been quoted as saying that "A DM only rolls the dice because of the noise they make", and while I have no idea if he really said that, you aren't in bad company if you prefer to roll behind a screen.

Difficult Terrain

Your party may encounter such things as deep mud, thin ice, carpets of grasping tendrils, or even slippery stone in your adventures. Different types of adverse conditions can spice up encounters, and lend unique challenges to different areas. Feel free to invent more types of terrain as it suits the story.

Terrain Type Effect
Brittle Brittle or unstable ground, such as when crossing thin ice or particularly large leaves, requires delicacy and careful foot placement. Each time a character takes a move action, they must take a Wits or Luck roll to find a sufficiently solid path to their destination without falling through. Each success grants a meter of safe movement, but each meter past that distance has a 50% chance of resulting in the character falling through.
Deep Deep terrain is ground that you naturally fall into, like deep snow or mud. It is heavy enough to slow you as you push through it to move. This wasted energy and movement slows down all move actions by some set number of meters. A field after a heavy rain might reduce everyone's movement speed by one meter, while a knee deep mud pond might reduce movement by two meters. Waste high water might reduce movement by three meters. Most of the time, you will always be able to move at least one meter.
Flowing When you begin your turn on or within flowing terrain, such as a river, or enter into it, you are immediately pushed some number of meters in the direction of the current. Weaker currents might not be enough to cause trouble in this way, but a moderate current in waist high water could cause a meter of movement. Deeper water, faster water, or mountain winds could cause three, five, or even more meters of movement. When pushed, you can roll Vigor, Grit or Agility to reduce the distance that you are carried by up to one meter per success.
Grasping When the ground is actively gripping or absorbing you, such as when you are standing in grasping tendrils, or in the trap of a greedbloom, you may need to make an active effort to tear yourself free each turn. When you take a move action that includes Grasping terrain, your move distance is limited to the lesser of either your agility or the number of successes you roll on a Vigor check.
Slippery Slippery ground makes it hard to stop, leading to characters sliding further than they intended. Whenever you attempt to come to a halt, or to change the direction that you're moving in, either by a move action, reaching the end of a knockback, or some other ability, you may keep sliding in your current direction. Normal ice can easily reach a point where it causes a base amount of three additional meters of slipping, but any amount of slipping is possible depending on the smoothness of the ground. You must roll Agility to reduce your extra slippage by one meter per success.
Uneven When moving into a space that is covered with debris, oddly shaped rocks, or other such uneven ground, it can be difficult to keep your footing. When you do so, you must roll Agility and get more successes than the difficulty of that space, or fall prone. Typically piles of rubble or garbage will have difficulties of one or two, but it is possible for things to be more difficult.

Customizing Armor

Armor

Staying alive is generally preferable to being dead, and staying healthy is generally preferable to having your guts full of whatever the world tried to put there today. For both of those aims, protective gear can help. Calculating the cost of protective gear comes down to this formula: Construction × Protection × Recovery, rounded up to the nearest Droma. Each factor increases or decreases the cash value of the protective gear. Creating armor from extravagant materials may increase the cost, and while warded armor can be of negligent weight, artificial armor weighs at least 3kg per point of armor.

Construction

Artificial armor consists of conspicuous plates of hardened material laid over padding, which is much simpler and more obvious than warded armor. Warded armor comes from enchantments that can be placed on nearly anything, and so doesn't need to look anything like armor. This concealment, as well as the additional difficulty in creating warded protective gear, makes it much more expensive. Bodily protection cannot be purchased, it is simply a part of your body, or not. If you have harvested the bodily protection of another character, it now counts as artificial gear.

Construction Cost Factor
Artificial 10D
Ward 40D

Protection

Gear can protect against as many conditions as the builder can provide for, but each increases the cost by a factor. Additionally, protective techniques may not have been developed for some afflictions, or may not be known to many. Some afflictions may require artificial gear, some may require warded gear.

Protects Against Cost Factor
Stress 1
Wounds 2
Exhaustion 1
Any Progressive Effect 1⁄3
Any Affliction 1

Example: Tor commissions some custom armor, asking for two points of Stress protection and three points of Wounds protection. Two points of Stress means a cost factor of two, plus six more from the three points of Wounds protection, for a total cost factor of 8.

Recovery

Professionally made protective gear can self-repair under various circumstances. While it is possible to acquire gear that does not, such things are seen as useless liabilities, like buying a canteen with a hole in it. Only one kind of recovery can be imbued into armor at a time.

Recovery Name Cost Factor Condition
No Recovery 1⁄2 The gear does not self-repair, making it dramatically less useful in the long term.
Daily Mending 1 The gear recovers one point of armor per day, at the same time of day.
Daily Recovery 2 The gear recovers all points of armor at the same time every day.
Quick-sealing 5 The gear recovers one point of armor every hour.
Unwavering 10 The gear recovers one point of armor at the start of each of the wearer's turns.

Customizing Weapons

Much like armor, the costs of weapons are also driven by a formula, and just like armor, we've opted to provide a variety of examples for players instead of showing them the formula right away. The cost of a weapon is very simple: 15D × Quality × Range Factor. Quality is simply the quality of the weapon, and the Range Factors are listed below. If you would like a longer range than 20 meters, consider learning some technomancy or sharpshooting abilities.

Range Cost Factor
Adjacent 1
Reach (up to 4 meters of additional reach) 2 per meter of reach beyond adjacent
Ranged (up to 20 meters) 4

House Rules

You and your players are welcome to modify the system to your own tastes. Even if the system was perfect, it still might not be perfect for your needs. Just make sure that everyone is on board with the changes.

Example house rules:

  • We are going to ignore encumbrance rules, because we don't feel that they add to the experience.
  • All traits on all characters are tripled, because rolling big piles of dice is fun.
  • There are no Arcane or Divine abilities in this world, because we want to play a more grounded game.
  • Everyone takes one Major action and two minor actions each turn, because we feel like that makes things flow better.
  • It will now take Wounds equal to ten times a character's Vigor to render them Helpless, and twenty times a character's Vigor to kill them, because we want fights to last a long time, and almost never result in death.
  • We're going to have two game masters, because we have seven players, and I've wanted to try running a game with my friend for a while now.
  • As the GM, I'm going to roll behind the screen so I can fudge dice without my players knowing if I did it.

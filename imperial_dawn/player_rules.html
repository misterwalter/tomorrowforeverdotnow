How to Play Imperial Dawn

As you begin to play, you will step into the role of a unique character that you yourself will create. You will act through this character from the beginning of their story to the end. Sometimes that story ends in success. Perhaps they seal up a seam in the fabric of the universe, or maybe they find love, or perhaps all they want is just to survive the day. It's also possible that they could be poisoned, eaten, blown to smithereens, and turned into a cloud of singing flies. It's a strange and harsh world, and while it's possible to thrive, you must always be ready for whatever the world brings you.

The Basics

This might look like a lot at first, but you can get started with just a few ideas, and pick up the rest as you go.

  • You can have your character try anything that you can think of, but they might not succeed at it.
  • Your character has six Traits and some Abilities.
    • Traits are a set of numbers that represent your general capability in six areas. three is bad, four is average, and six is great.
    • Abilities are specific things that you can reliably do.
  • When you try to do a thing, the GM may have you roll one die for each point you have in your most relevant Trait.
  • When you roll, count one "success" for each die that lands with a three or higher.
  • The more successes the better. It takes two to accomplish something moderately difficult. Neither the number of dice rolled, nor the actual numbers on the dice have any impact on gameplay.
  • When timing matters, like in combat, you have one major action and one minor action per turn. When this is true, a few more rules kick in that generally don't matter otherwise.
    • Entering a Stance ability takes a major action, and once you're in it you stay in it until you end it, become Grappled, Helpless, Prone, or unconscious.
    • You can stay in a Stance for up to a minute, then you naturally fall out of it, though you can take another major action to re-enter it immediately.
    • Stances are exhausting and exiting one results in you taking one point of Exhaustion.
    • Active abilities can only be used on your turn, reactions can be used at any time, passives are always in effect.
    • You can spend an action, major or minor, to move a number of meters up to your Agility. You can use both of your actions this way.
    • If you have Wounds equal to or exceeding twice your Vigor, you're Helpless. Four times your Vigor kills you.
  • In the case of conflict between this and the full rules, the full rules take precedence.

The Dice

While the players and the game master control what characters attempt to do, the dice adjudicate if those characters succeed. While a roll is not required for every action, one should generally roll whenever there is a significant chance of failure. Whenever a roll is required, you should roll as many six-sided dice as you have points in your most relevant trait. So if a character with 3 Vigor attempts to kick down a door, they would roll three dice. This is true regardless of whether the character is controlled by one of the players or the game master.

Checks, Saves, Attacks, Clashes

All dice, including the game master's dice, must be rolled in the open for all involved to see, unless the results would be beyond the immediate perception of the players' characters, and even in this case the game master must still be able to see them. Once correctly rolled, the direct results cannot be altered by anyone, including the Game Master. Remember that the trait used reflects not just the obstacle, but your approach to it as well. You wouldn't kick down a door with Wits, and you wouldn't pick the lock with Vigor.

Vigor Kicking down a door Punching 'em in the face Resisting the effects of poison Arm wrestling
Wits Noticing something quickly Launching shards of Glassamancy Seeing the contradictions in a lie Finding a needle in a haystack first
Grit Giving a tough love talk to a cowardly cadet Berating said cadet to make them panic Resisting fear Staring contest

Checks

If a character is actively doing something that isn't directly opposed by another character, then it's a Check. Checks get one success per die that lands with a 3 or higher face up. Not all rolls are the same difficulty. For example, picking a discount padlock is much less difficult than cracking a high security safe. Checks are therefore assigned a difficulty by the GM before the player actually rolls them. The GM does not necessarily need to share this difficulty with the player, but the player should typically have a general idea of their character's ability to accomplish the task.

  • Easy checks have a difficulty of one.
  • Normal checks have a difficulty of two.
  • Challenging checks have a difficulty of three.
  • Hard checks have a difficulty of four.

If the number of successes rolled is:

  • Less than the difficulty: You fail to perform the action, take a very long time to do so, or incur some other significant penalty along the way.
  • Greater than or equal to the difficulty: You succeed and there may be additional effects. In most cases this should be considered a flawless success, increasing in degree as more extra successes pile up.

Some checks may be even more difficult, there is no upper limit. A character that attempts to punch a mountain in half is welcome to try, but the difficulty for such a thing would be in the tens of thousands. It does not matter what percentage of the dice were successes, only the number of successes.

Attacks and Defenses

When one character tries to do something to another character, and the second character attempts to defend themselves, we call this an attack. Both characters roll once, or all characters if the attacking character is targeting multiple characters. The Attacker's rolled successes are usually denoted by X. Many abilities key off of this X value.

X Result
X < Defenders Successes The attack fails. If you were loosing an arrow at the target, they dodge it. If you were lying to them, they catch on.
X >= Defenders Successes The attack succeeds. If you were loosing an arrow at the target, you hit them. If you were lying to them, they fall for it.

Basic Attacks

Most physical melee attacks in Imperial Dawn are modeled as either Vigor or Agility attacks against the targets Agility. Ranged physical attacks tend to be Agility or Wits against the target's Agility, depending on the weapon used. They can inflict Wounds, Stress, Daze, or any other status effect that seems reasonable on a hit, even a glancing blow.

Example: Tor slashes at a construct with their scythe. Tor rolls five dice for their five Vigor, and the construct rolls three dice for its three Agility. Tor gets three successes, and the construct gets three. Tor hits.

Example: Tor brings the rubble of the construct back to town, and tries to convince the scrap dealer that it is from an ancient artifact. Tor rolls four dice due to having four Charm, and the scrap dealer rolls four dice due to having four Wits. Tor gets one success, and the scrap dealer gets two successes. The scrap dealer therefore feels suspicious of Tor, and decides to refuse to pay a premium.

Clashes

Sometimes two or more characters roll, each actively trying to do something that directly contradicts the active actions of another character. In this case, characters roll checks and whoever gets the most successes wins. Ties resolve as ties in the story as well.

Example: Tor and the scrap dealer each complain about the other to the local chamber of commerce. Tor is more charming, and rolls four dice instead of the scrap dealer's three. They both get three successes on the first roll, and so they get a tie. The chamber gives them a chance to resolve things between themselves. Tor insists on a judgment, and so they roll again. Tor gets no successes, and the scrap dealer gets two. The chamber sides with the scrap dealer.

Rolling Together

Sometimes things require multiple turns or people to complete successfully. These fall into a few different categories.

Additive Challenge

When there is simply a lot to do, it is sometimes possible to combine the efforts of multiple players across multiple turns. In this case, add the successes together as characters roll them.

Example: Tor and their accomplice hear the lock opening in the scrap dealer's warehouse, and start rolling Agility to frantically grab everything that isn't nailed down in a jewelry shop before the owner arrives.

Assistance

Other times it might be more sensible to simply grant the character with the most dice a bonus die for each other assisting character.

Example: Tor attempts to barricade the door with a chair, and their accomplice helps them to position it.

Liability

Characters might sometimes just get in each other's way. In some scenarios, only the lower roll is counted.

Example: Tor and their accomplice both attempt to hide in the room. Tor rolls well and finds a good spot, but their accomplice is quickly spotted.

Critical Success

Whenever you roll a trait, if you roll successes on every single die, fortune turns your way. In that case, you can roll additional dice equal to the lesser of either the trait you were rolling initially, or your Luck score. Any successes are considered part of the original roll, and these additional dice cannot trigger another critical success.

How Much do I do?

Many abilities say that they apply or remove X points of some condition, or they mention a specific trait, like Vigor, and therefore do Vigor points of something. The X value is the number of successes that the attacker rolled, including dice gained from an item's quality score. If a specific trait is put in place of a number, that means that the acting character's trait should be added to the quality of any item being used. So an ability that creates Luck frogs would create four frogs for a character with three Luck and a Quality one focus, and nine frogs for a character with six Luck and a quality three focus.

Typically a basic physical attack, like swinging a sword at someone, will inflict a number of Wounds on the target equal to the trait used to attack. Weapons, focuses, abilities or other effects might modify this.

Abilities

Abilities represent special skills and powers that a character has. They can generally be used at any time, so long as the user is not Helpless, and has enough freedom of movement to perform them. Abilities are the bread and butter of any adventurer's toolkit and are usually gained via leveling up.

Ability Types

Type Mechanic
Active Active abilities can only be used on a character's turn, and consume either a major or minor action, depending on the ability type.
Passive Passive abilities are always in effect, so long as the character is alive.
Reaction Reaction abilities can be used in certain circumstances outlined in the ability itself. They do not take an action, and do not need to be used on a character's turn. Typically they can even be used when caught off guard, as they are considered to be reflexive. Reaction abilities can only be used if you haven't used one since the end of your last turn.
Stance Stances take a major action to enter, but once they are entered, they are constantly in effect until the character enters another stance, becomes grappled, helpless, prone, or unconscious, or chooses to stop performing the stance at any time during their turn. You can stay in a stance for up to a minute, then you naturally fall out of it, though you can take another major action to re-enter it immediately. Stances are exhausting and exiting one results in you taking one point of Exhaustion.

Ability Details

Whenever two instances of the same ability would affect something at the same time, both effects exist, but only the stronger version has an effect. So a character that is warned of an incoming fireball by two allies with "Get Down!", one that grants them one additional die, and one that grants them three, will gain three additional dice on their defense, not one or four. Note that this does not apply to conditions such as Wounds, Daze, etc, which can stack on a character utterly regardless of their source.

The italic text in abilities is not actual rules text, rather it is there to give advice, examples, and flavor regarding the game and the world.

Categories

Abilities are divided into a few broad categories. While the categories have no mechanical meaning after character creation, they serve to organize the different trees of abilities, and to explain how the abilities actually work.

Category Explanation
Arcane Magical talents that are powered and understood by the user. They tend to focus around ideas that play out on an individual scale.
Divine Connections to universal forces, rather than mortal concerns. These effects tend to be the result of the favor of greater forces.
Species Bodily capabilities that are reflected in the character's bodies. Most characters inherit some of these, though many develop more over time.
Martial Physical combat skills that are learned from training and experience.
Skills and Trades Knowledge and skills that are useful for a variety of hobbies, jobs, and field work.

Trees

Trees are branching structures of abilities that a character can learn. Examples include Hemomancy, Alchemy, and Finesse. Abilities may require that a character learn other abilities within a tree before taking them, but there are no other prerequisites. There is no cost to "entering" a tree, and no limit on how many you can enter, besides the three Category rule at character creation.

Reflavoring

We've built this system to be as flexible as possible, but all systems have their limitations. If you have a character concept that you enjoy, but that isn't directly included in the game, think about how you might tweak existing content to get to it. Glassamancy easily turns into ice magic, technomantic cannons become advanced fire breath with just a little more effort. You are always free to put another coat of paint on the mechanics that we have devised in order to make them better fit your story. Changing mechanics is a little trickier, but as long as you talk to your GM about what would be fair, you can usually work something out.

Characters

Characters in Imperial Dawn come in two forms: Full characters and minion characters. Most story important characters will be full characters, and players will pretty much always play as full characters, though they may have minion followers.

Full Characters

Full Characters are generated using the rules outlined in Character Creation and generally have full access to developing and leveling up using a character sheet.

Player Character Creation

When people come together to play Imperial Dawn, or most tabletop RPGs for that matter, all but one of them will be a player. Players typically each control one character, and experience the game world through the lens of that character. That character is created with the following steps:

  1. Think broadly about what kind of character you'd like to play, in terms of their personality and capabilities.
  2. Name your character, and determine their personality, their life up until the game's story begins, and other biographical details.
  3. Skim through the abilities available until something catches your eye. Don't worry about learning all of the abilities, or even all of the trees. You don't need to.
  4. Select a species that your character is a member of. This can be any of the species mentioned, or a combination thereof.
    • If your character is a member of only one species, then you automatically gain two abilities from that species.
    • If you are making a character that is of two species, select one ability from each species.
    • If you are making a character of more than two species, pick two species to be the ones that initially affect you mechanically and use those two. You are free to use one of your three starting abilities to pick an ability from a third species, and you can continue to pick more species abilities as you advance.
  5. Select three starting abilities, each from different categories that are not the Traits category. Eligible categories are Arcane, Divine, Martial, Skills, and Species. Characters are always able to take Species abilities just like any other ability.
  6. Select your traits. You start the game with all of your traits set to three. You can then add a +2 bonus to one trait, and a +1 bonus two other different traits. Once complete, all of your traits should be at most five, and at least three.
  7. Pick out a few items for your inventory. You start with the following:
    • A focus of Quality one with one perk.
    • Either one Quality 1 item or protective gear that grants one point of armor.

Feel free to take these steps out of order, so long as the character you end up with could still be generated by this exact method. There really isn't a correct order to do this in, and everyone does this in a different order. If you can't find abilities that fit the character you'd like to play, consider reflavoring existing abilities, or letting us know on our discord.

The Party Gains Levels

Most parties begin at level five, because starting characters have five abilities when made with the rules above.

As the party travels, practices their talents, and learns about the world, they grow in skill. At the start of each session, your GM will select two of the following objectives, and you will, as a party, select two more. Whenever the party does something that matches one of the currently selected objectives, it is marked as completed. When you have completed six different objectives, your party levels up, and all objectives are reset. The GM may wish to alter the number of objectives selected or required, or they may wish to alter the list of possible objectives, so they better fit the game that they are trying to run. They may even give a bonus objective on the spot if something especially brilliant occurs.

Example: Tor finally made amends with the scrap dealer, settling a long standing feud and allowing both to go back to their lives. This was a great character moment that didn't quite fit any of the other objectives so the GM awarded them a bonus objective for it.

Name Requirement
Day Off Accomplish no other objectives.
Get Paid Get rewarded by someone else for completing something.
Greet Make a new friend or enemy.
Lore Learn something significant about the history of an important person, place, or thing.
Mystery Learn something that was known to very few or no one at all.
Plot Point Pass a plot point. (Only the GM can select this)
Revelation Learn something important and new about a player character or close ally.

Winners Make a gamble, literally or metaphorically, that pays off despite the odds being against it.

Characters Gain Abilities

Characters gain one ability each time they level up. After character creation, there are no restrictions on what categories or ability trees a character may draw from while leveling up. However, it is required that characters have all the prerequisite abilities for any ability that they wish to take.

Catching up

When a character has a lower number of abilities than their party's level, they can gain one ability in between each session. They must have all of the prerequisite abilities for the ability that they want to take.

Size Categories

Different characters can be wildly different sizes, but each falls into one size category determined by their bulk. While weight generally correlates with bulk for obvious reasons, there are tiny characters that are exceedingly heavy, and huge characters that weigh almost nothing at all. For the purposes of laying out characters on a map with 1m by 1m spaces, the sizes are as follows:

Size Grid Space Required
Miniature 1⁄4m diameter (4 can share)
Tiny 1⁄3m diameter (3 can share)

Medium 1m diameter
Large 2m diameter
Huge 3m diameter
Behemoth 4m diameter
Gargantuan 5m+ diameter

Minion Characters

Minions are side characters that just don't merit a full character sheet. They have only one stat: Dice. Their Traits are all assumed to be equal to their Dice. Whenever they take a point of Wounds, Stress, or some other effect that could eliminate them from play, they lose a point of Dice instead. When they hit zero dice, they are killed, run off, or otherwise removed from the story. Healing minions restores their Dice, but not past their maximum. If a minion becomes more important later on, they can always be promoted to a full character by the GM. They can have abilities, but they generally don't have more than one or two.

Action

Taking Turns

By default, everyone simply narrates their characters' actions in whatever order is convenient, but when the action in game gets faster paced, the rules get a little more structured to keep the story coherent. Typically this happens in a fight or chase, but the Game Master can declare that turns will be used or not at any time. The length of a turn is "a few seconds", since these situations are often chaotic and unpredictable. The rules for turns in Imperial Dawn are simple:

  • Individual characters don't have turns, teams have turns, and characters can take actions in whatever order they like within their team's turn. That said, just going clockwise from the GM for the most part is highly recommended, especially in larger groups.
  • Characters can take one minor action and one major action on their team's turn. Any time that you could take a major action, you can take a minor action instead.
  • Characters can freely repeat major and minor actions, so long as they spend the actions to do so. For example, moving twice on the same turn by using both actions is acceptable and commonplace.
  • Characters have one reaction that they can spend to use a Reaction ability. This reaction is restored at the start of their turn.
  • There is no limit to the number of passive abilities that can be called upon at any time, nor any limit on the number of times that a passive ability can be used.
  • Characters can ready actions to occur on their opponent's turns. They need only describe the exact action that they will take, and the conditions upon which they will take it. Doing so requires expending the action or actions before the readied actions would be used, and can result in wasting actions waiting for something to happen. Readied actions are taken immediately without any further thought the first time a situation would trigger them. Reaction abilities don't require readied actions, they are trained reflexes.

Initiating Combat

When a fight breaks out, one side often attacks the other in a coordinated or surprising way that means that they will go first. However, there are times when both sides may be expecting a fight. In those cases, one character from each side should clash with each other on Wits, with the winner rallying their team to take the initiative and the first turn. In this case, ties should be rerolled.

Facing

Characters' facing is also tracked in combat and at other times when it may be significant. Facing defines three areas around a character:

  • Front: Everything in a 90 degree cone in front of a character is in Front of the character.
  • Back: Everything in a 90 degree cone directly behind a character is considered the back.
  • Sides: Everything else is on the Sides of the character.

Whenever a character takes a move action, they can freely change their facing for the remainder of their turn, but they cannot otherwise change their facing unless otherwise specified. Basic attacks and most abilities can only target a creature in front of you, but some abilities may stem from the user's sides or back. Other abilities may key off of the facing of the target instead of the user.

When an ability involves a minion, the ability's user decides the minion's facing.

When a minion uses an ability, the minion's commander decides the minion's facing.

Movement

Moving with a minor action

Characters can move with a minor action. Whenever they do so, they walk, run, or otherwise move up to their Agility in meters. This movement does not need to be in a straight line, nor does it need to be all at once, so a character can move, perform an action, and move back or continue moving so long as they don't move too far. Basic movement suffers from the usual limitations around walking and running in that you can only traverse solid ground, instead of freely walking on walls, ceilings, water, or thin air. However numerous opportunities exist to do just those things with the right items, abilities, or other such enhancements.

When moving on a rectangular grid, diagonal movement is considered to be one meter of movement, and diagonal spaces are considered adjacent.

Difficult Terrain

Be aware that you may encounter such things as deep mud, thin ice, carpets of grasping tendrils, or even slippery stone in your adventures. Different types of adverse conditions can have different adverse effects on those trying to traverse them. Your GM will give you the details, just be ready to improvise around these effects.

Climbing

All characters can climb to some degree, but not all can do it well. When climbing a reasonably grip-able object, such as a tree or pile of boulders, you must take a move action, and then roll an Agility check, moving one meter for each success that you receive. If you roll a zero on that check, you lose your grip and fall. Some surfaces may be particularly easy, difficult, or dangerous to traverse, which could cause you to reroll successes or failures, face the difficult terrain effects above, or have some other unique effect. While climbing you are assumed to have one hand and both feet busy stabilizing yourself at all times, though you can get a hand or leg free for an action with a successful Agility roll. If you are knocked prone, then you immediately fall from whatever you are climbing.

Falling and Landing Damage

Falling great heights is perfectly safe in Imperial Dawn, however landing can be quite painful. Characters fall at the end of their turns, assuming that they are not on top of or attached to something. Upon landing from a fall, characters take one point of Wounds for each meter of this turn's continuous falling after the first. Characters who are neither Helpless, Grappled, nor otherwise restrained from moving can also roll an Agility save. They then reduce Wounds taken by one for each success on that save. Armor that protects against Wounds applies against injuries sustained from falling.

While most falls will not be anywhere near as long, characters can fall up to 150 meters on their first turn of freefall. They fall up to 250 meters per turn after that. We humbly recommend not falling that far.

Example

Berguld leaps out of a second story window to avoid Tor chopping through their door. He falls 4 meters, and rolls 2 successes out of his four agility dice. This means that he kinda sticks the landing and only takes two wounds instead of the maximum of four.

Flying

Characters cannot fly by default, but some abilities, items, or other effects may grant the ability to fly. If you can fly, then when you use a move action, you can move freely as if you were walking in any direction, including straight up, straight down, or at any other angle. You can choose to end a movement in midair, which will allow you to hover in place and not fall as you normally would at the end of your turn.

If you are knocked prone you lose the ability to fly on your next turn. If you are in the air, you then fall. Keep in mind that landing damage can be significant, so always consider the risks when leaving the ground.

Forced Movement

When you are forced to move a distance, you immediately move that distance. If you hit a solid object that you won't break through, you take one wound for every meter of remaining forced movement. Armor can reduce wounds taken in this way. If you suffer Wounds from such an impact you fall prone.

Playing with The Game Master

The role of game master is typically taken by only one person, and that person is responsible for every aspect of the game world that is not the player's characters. The game master therefore writes the world ahead of time, sometimes riffing off of an established setting or work of fiction and sometimes not, and controls all of the non-player characters in the world. They also interpret the results of dice rolls, and are the final arbiter of any disagreement. This is a lot of responsibility, and it must be used well. Their rules are expounded upon in another section. You can read that page or not, just keep the following in mind:

  • The GM does a lot of work to try to make a good story and setting for you, so bring them snacks, thank them from time to time, and put some effort in yourself when the opportunity arises. It makes a huge difference for the GM.
  • Tell the GM what you want, and accept that you may not get it right away.
  • Put effort into your character, and tell the GM about what you've come up with. It will give them more to work with, and they'll be able to better fit the story to you and your character.
  • You are in ultimate control of the story, because at the end of the day, you decide what your character does. You decide your approach to the problems that your character faces.
  • You can propose house rules, and you have a say in what kind of game is played. The GM may run the rest of the world, but they're only one person at the table.

Tips for Players

  • Have a Character, Not Just a Stat Block: The game is more fun when you're controlling a character with personality. Even if that character is quite a bit like you, it's good to have an idea of who you are playing, not just what. Some people write backstories, others just cue off of a certain feeling or joke. Try some things, and figure out what works for you.
  • Have a Plan: Try to avoid running on autopilot as much as you can. A good game master will build a world, or at least a scenario instead of a series of forced encounters, and you should always be thinking about how best to handle that scenario. Don't trust the old sages that you're gathering artifacts for? Go investigate their vault! Losing a fight? Set the building on fire, or drop your weapons and negotiate. The strength of tabletop RPGs is their flexibility, so use it!
  • Never Fight Fair: Combat in Imperial Dawn is much more dangerous than in most other games, so if you can't avoid it, make sure that you have as many advantages as possible.
  • Exploit Your Abilities: Imperial Dawn was designed with a sort of two-tiered balance in mind. All abilities are intended to be usable and useful in a straightforward manner, but clever characters can often make themselves dramatically more effective by finding combinations and situations that multiply their effectiveness.
  • Don't Betray the Party: There is an element of social contract in these games. While a betrayal may occasionally pay off into a great story moment, it usually doesn't. Whatever your intentions, it's a sure recipe for resentment between friends, especially if characters suffer greatly or die as a result. While it may someday be necessary for you to break this rule, do so with extreme caution.
  • Know Your Table: Some games are more serious, some are more goofy. Some people like grimdark, some like heroics. Some like lots of crunchy combats that push players tactically, some don't care for fighting. Every play group is different, and you should try to meet your group part way wherever you are. Imperial Dawn is an extremely flexible system, and while that's good, it can lead to largely incompatible characters starting off on an adventure together. Play how you like, just make sure that all of the players are on board, even if their characters aren't.
